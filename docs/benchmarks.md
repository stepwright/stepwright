## Reading the numbers

Both columns ran on the same Apple M4 Max (12 performance and 4 efficiency
cores): natively under macOS with Apple Clang, and in Docker Desktop's Linux VM
with GCC 14. Compare stepwright with the baseline *in the same column*; the columns
differ in compiler and operating system, not in hardware.

**What is fast.**
- *Steps.* A `then` step costs about 2 ns (2.6 ns with GCC), an `after` on a child
  that completes at once about 4–5 ns, a park/wake pair about 8 ns: two to five
  function-pointer calls' worth, with no allocation. `std::function` is 1 ns per
  call, a plain function pointer 0.7 ns: stepwright's price for explicit,
  inspectable steps over a bare callback is about 1 ns per step.
- *Against coroutines.* A coroutine's body is straight-line code, so sequencing is
  free (0.25–0.71 ns per loop step against stepwright's 2 ns), and awaiting a child
  whose frame the compiler elides costs 1.4 ns (Clang). Without elision a child
  coroutine allocates its frame: 10–17 ns per child (`frames_per_item = 1`), 3–4×
  stepwright's `after`. Clang elides only when it can see the child; **GCC 14 never
  elides here** (`BM_coro_after_sync_child` allocates one frame per child on
  Linux). stepwright's children are members and never allocate, whatever the
  compiler can see.
- *Primitives.* An uncontended `async_mutex` lock/unlock (3.2–3.7 ns) is cheaper
  than `std::mutex` on both systems; a channel send+pull on one thread is 3 ns
  against 8–11 ns for `std::mutex` + `std::deque`.
- *Thread pool.* Posting from outside costs 7–24 ns per item against 53 ns to
  1.6 µs (macOS) or 7.8 µs (Linux) for the textbook mutex + condition-variable
  pool at 8 threads, whose single lock collapses under contention. Re-posting
  from workers scales linearly: about 4 ns per item on one worker, 0.56–0.61 ns
  aggregate on eight.
- *Fan-out* (M5 target). A `when_all_range` of 10,000 trivial machines costs about
  25 ns per machine on 8 workers against 18–20 ns on one (1.26× on Linux, 1.38× on
  macOS; target ≤ 1.5×), down from 228–381 ns on 8 workers before M5. With about
  1 µs of work per leaf, 8 workers are 6.7× (Linux) to 8.5× (macOS) faster than
  one (target ≥ 5×). `BM_leaf_work` shows the leaf's work itself: about 0.97 µs
  with Apple Clang and 0.57 µs with GCC, which compiles the same loop better; the speed-up is measured
  against the same work on one worker, so it compares like with like.

**What is not fast, and why.**
- *Trivial fan-out does not get faster with more workers.* One worker starts all
  10,000 children and posts each to its own queue; the others steal. With leaves
  that do nothing, that single starter is the bottleneck, and each extra worker
  adds cache-line traffic on the starter's queue and on the children it has just
  touched. 8 workers stay within 1.4× of one, but they cannot beat it: fan-out
  pays off when leaves do work (the `_work` variant).
- *Cross-thread wake-ups* cost what the hardware costs: a park/wake round trip
  between two threads is 150–260 ns, dominated by moving cache lines between cores
  (and, in the VM, by its scheduler).
- *`when_all` bookkeeping* is about 4 ns per child against 1.9–2.7 ns for a
  hand-written atomic countdown: the difference pays for cancellation (a token for
  every child, first-error-wins, structured completion). Since M5 a large
  `when_all_range` counts completions in 16 counters, which makes the
  one-thread case about 0.2 ns per child slower and the contended case an order of
  magnitude faster.
- *Thread-to-thread streams* (`BM_channel_spsc_threads`,
  `BM_channel_bounded_backpressure`) and the outside-poster pool benchmarks depend
  on how the operating system schedules two busy threads; they vary by up to 2×
  between sessions (see the method notes in `benchmarks/README.md`). Desktop
  processes kept two to three cores of the Mac busy during the macOS run (load
  average in the platform table); the Linux VM's load average does not show them.
